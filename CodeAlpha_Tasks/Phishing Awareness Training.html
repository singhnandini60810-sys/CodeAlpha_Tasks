Phishing awareness training

Phishing is when an attacker pretends to be someone you trust to get you to click, log in, pay, or share something. This 15-minute module shows you how to spot it, why it works on smart people, and what to do next.

Recognizing phishing emails

No single sign proves an email is fake. Look for a cluster of them.

The sender

Check the real address, not the display name. Look for lookalike domains (rn for m, extra words, odd endings like .co).

The greeting

"Dear User" or "Dear Customer" from a company that knows your name is a warning.

The pressure

Deadlines, threats and rewards that push you to act before you think.

The links

Hover (or long-press on mobile) to see the true destination before clicking.

The attachment

Unexpected ZIP, HTML, or Office files asking you to enable macros are high risk.

The request

Legitimate organizations don't ask for passwords, OTPs or card PINs by email or chat.

Recognizing fake websites

Read a web address from right to left, starting at the first single slash. The part just before the first "/" and before the final dots is the domain that owns the page.

https://login.bank.com.secure-verify.net/account

Here the real domain is secure-verify.net. "bank.com" is only a decoy subdomain.

Lookalike domains

paypa1.com, arnazon.com, micros0ft.com. Characters swapped for similar ones.

The padlock is not proof

HTTPS only means the connection is encrypted. Phishing sites use it too.

Other tells

Poor spelling, mismatched logos, a login form that appears after clicking a message link, and a page that asks for far more than a login.

Safer habit: don't use the link in the message. Type the site's address yourself or use your bookmark.

Social engineering tactics

Attackers exploit emotions and habits, not just technology.

TacticWhat it sounds likeWhy it works
Urgency"Act within 24 hours or lose access."Time pressure shuts down careful checking.
Authority"This is the CEO. I need this transfer now."People hesitate to question bosses, police, or IT.
Fear"Suspicious sign-in detected. Confirm your identity."Worry makes us click to make it stop.
Greed / reward"You've won a gift card. Claim now."The promise of a prize lowers suspicion.
Curiosity"Is this you in this video?"We want to know what's behind the link.
Helpfulness"Can you quickly buy gift cards for me?"We want to be good colleagues.

Not just email: smishing is phishing by SMS, vishing by phone call, spear phishing targets a specific person with researched details, and BEC (business email compromise) impersonates executives or vendors to redirect payments.

Real-world examples

Fake invoices to Google and Facebook (2013–2015)

A scammer posed as a hardware vendor and sent convincing invoices and emails, collecting over $100 million before being caught.

Lesson: verify payment-detail changes through a known phone number.

Twitter hack (July 2020)

Attackers phoned employees pretending to be internal IT and got credentials for internal tools, then took over high-profile accounts to run a bitcoin scam.

Lesson: phone calls can be phishing too. Call IT back on the official number.

Fake Google security alert (2016)

A campaign chairman's staff received a "someone has your password" email with a link to a fake Google sign-in page. The account's emails were later leaked.

Lesson: never use the link in a security alert. Go to the account directly and enable multi-factor authentication.

Best practices

  • Pause. If a message creates urgency, slow down. That feeling is the signal.
  • Verify out of band. Contact the sender using a number or site you already trust.
  • Hover before you click. Check where links and buttons really lead.
  • Use multi-factor authentication everywhere, ideally an authenticator app or security key, so a stolen password isn't enough.
  • Use a password manager. It won't autofill on a fake domain, which is a built-in alarm.
  • Never share OTPs, PINs, or passwords with anyone who contacts you first.
  • Keep software updated and don't open unexpected attachments.
  • Report, don't just delete. Use your organization's "report phishing" option or forward to your security team.

If you already clicked

Don't panic and don't hide it. Disconnect from the network if you downloaded a file, change the password for any account you typed it into (and anywhere you reused it), and tell your security team right away. Speed is what limits the damage.
